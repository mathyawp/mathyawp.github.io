Why continuity of φ is the hypothesis

The theorem (Rudin 6.11). If f is integrable with respect to α, with m ≤ f ≤ M, and φ is continuous on [m, M], then φ ∘ f is integrable too. The proof leans on uniform continuity: [m, M] is compact, so a single δ serves the whole range at once, and every interval on which f oscillates by less than δ has its composite oscillation controlled for free. The remaining intervals — where f still swings wide — carry only a small total weight, and φ is bounded there.

The counterexample. Take Thomae's f with φ = 1 above 0. Thomae's function is integrable — it is discontinuous only at the rationals — but φ jumps exactly at u = 0, the value f takes at every irrational. The composite is 1 on the rationals and 0 on the irrationals: Dirichlet's function, with oscillation 1 on every interval. The lower box fills solid and stays that way.

The hypothesis is sufficient, not necessary. Try φ = 1 above ½. It is just as discontinuous, so the theorem says nothing — yet each f here meets the level u = ½ only finitely often, so the composite has finitely many jumps and survives. A jump in φ only bites where f keeps returning to it.

Continuity sets the rate, too. On Thomae's f, φ(u) = u² drives the gap down faster than f's own, while φ(u) = √u — continuous but not Lipschitz at 0 — drives it down more slowly. Both converge. Continuity is all the theorem asks for.
